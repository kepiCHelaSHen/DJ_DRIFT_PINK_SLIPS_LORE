### The Night Speedball Learned (Fall 1957)

By autumn 1957, Speedball had won six straight races and started believing the streak meant something permanent. That happens to young racers eventually. He respected nobody over thirty. Especially not Jack Drummond.

It built slowly. Little comments: "Guess old timing still works sometimes." "You tune good for a dinosaur." "Heard Jack was quick once." Jack ignored all of it. Until the night Speedball crossed the line — in Jack's own garage.

Speedball brought his car in after grenading second gear. Jack spent three hours helping diagnose the transmission while Speedball hovered nearby talking too much. Then Speedball looked at the old framed racing photo on the wall — Jack in 1948 beside a chopped Comet Deuce covered in dirt and victory decals. He smirked: "That really you?" Jack nodded. Speedball laughed: "No offense, Jack... but I can't picture you beating anybody nowadays."

The garage went dead quiet. Tommy Pierce was there. He later said: "That's the closest I ever came to watching somebody accidentally start their own funeral."

Then Speedball added: "Maybe legends just sound faster because everybody else got old too."

Jack stared at him. Then: "You free Saturday?"

Saturday night. Cold air, clear sky, the strip overflowing. Even Henderson showed up early. Jack rolled into staging driving the old black '34 Comet Deuce — the same car from the photograph. Speedball laughed when he saw it. That stopped after the burnout, because the Deuce sounded vicious. Not modern fast. Violent fast. The kind of machine that wanted to kill whoever drove it.

Flag dropped. Speedball launched hard — too hard. Rear tires spun before catching. Jack's launch was almost lazy. Perfect. Controlled. Efficient. By sixty feet the Deuce was ahead. Speedball kept chasing harder — wrong instinct. His car drifted near the groove edge while the Deuce stayed planted like it ran on rails.

Jack crossed first by nearly two full car lengths.

The crowd didn't scream. They roared. People weren't celebrating a win. They were witnessing proof. Proof the old stories were real.

Speedball walked over afterward. Removed his gloves. "I was outta line." Jack studied him. "Yeah." Long silence. Then Speedball asked: "How the hell are you still that fast?" Jack leaned against the Deuce and answered: "Because I stopped trying to prove I was fast a long time ago."

This is the defining event of Dennis Kohl's life. The night a six-race win streak, a fast mouth, and a metallic green Comet met a '34 Deuce and a man who had nothing left to prove.

The apology was real — "I was outta line" — but the lesson goes deeper. Does Speedball carry it forward, or does he go back to being the kid who thinks a streak means something permanent? [INFERRED]
