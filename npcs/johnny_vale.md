# Johnny Vale — "Last Call"

## Identity
- **Age at death**: ~28
- **DOB**: ~1925
- **Died**: Late 1953, Route 9 west of Winterberry Acres
- **Car**: Pearl white 1949 Comet Rocket 88, hand-painted gold lettering: "LAST CALL"
- **Job**: Aircraft plant west of Riverside
- **Lived**: Tiny apartment above a feed store in Greenfield
- **Tier**: Former King of the Strip (deceased)
- **Token**: None (historical figure, not a living NPC — but referenced in many NPCs' dialogue)

## Backstory
Johnny Vale came back from the Pacific different. Not mean exactly. Just hollowed out around the edges. Whatever he saw in the war burned something out of him and left an engine in its place. He worked days at the aircraft plant and spent every spare dollar making the Rocket 88 faster. No wife. No kids. The car was the only thing in his life he truly cared about.

By 1950 he was unbeatable on the backroads. Not just fast. Untouchable. For three years, nobody could touch him. People still talk about the way that Rocket sounded coming down Route 9 at night. Deep. Angry. Like distant thunder rolling across the valley.

Gene Hartwell once said: "Some cars got horsepower. Johnny's car had hate in it." Nobody laughed when he said it.

Johnny wasn't cruel, but he was dangerous because he never lifted. That was the thing everybody remembers. Most racers lifted eventually. Johnny never did. Not for curves. Not for weather. Not for common sense.

## The Night Henderson Came

The night Henderson arrived in Winterberry Acres, nobody even knew who he was. Just some outsider from San Bernardino towing a black Comet Drifter behind a dented Atlas pickup. Quiet. Didn't brag. Didn't drink coffee with the crowd. Didn't smile. Carmine almost turned him away from the runway races because he thought Henderson looked like trouble.

The grudge race happened two Fridays later. Winner takes the gate. Winner gets the crown. Winner gets respect. Everybody in Potter County showed up. People parked clear down Airport Road. Kids climbed the fairground fences. Mel ran out of burgers by seven-thirty. Nicky Ferris made more money that night than he ever admitted.

Johnny staged first. White Rocket 88 idling hard enough to shake dust off the bleachers. Then Henderson rolled up beside him in the black Drifter. No chrome. No lettering. Flat black primer. Steel wheels. Mean little headlights. People laughed. Until they heard it idle.

Wes Buckley: "That car sounded wrong. Like it had bad intentions."

The flag dropped. Johnny cut a near-perfect launch. Half track — dead even. By the big end — Henderson started pulling. Not by much. Just enough. Johnny lost by less than half a car length.

Silence hit the strip. Nobody had ever seen Johnny lose clean before.

Johnny demanded another run. Then another. Then another. By midnight he'd lost four straight. Something in him cracked.

The old-timers tell the rest differently depending on how much they've had to drink. Some say Johnny accused Henderson of cheating. Some say he shoved him. Some say Henderson never said a single word the entire night.

## The Crash

Johnny left angry. Too angry. He tore out of the fairgrounds alone sometime after one in the morning and headed west on Route 9 toward Riverside. Rain had started coming down. Not hard. Just enough to make the blacktop slick.

About three miles past Miller Farm, there's a long downhill bend where the road dips near the creek. Johnny took the curve too fast. The Rocket 88 clipped the shoulder, spun sideways, rolled twice, and slammed roof-first into a sycamore tree.

They found him just before dawn. Still in the driver's seat. Hands gripping the wheel so hard they had to pry his fingers loose.

The car almost looked repairable. Johnny didn't.

## The Funeral

Half the racers in Potter County attended. Henderson stood in the back the entire time wearing a black suit nobody had ever seen before or since. Never spoke. Never approached the family. Never explained himself.

After that night, nobody called Henderson "the new guy" anymore. He became the king of the strip.

## Legacy

Johnny Vale is the ghost that hangs over the Winterberry Acres racing scene. His name comes up in specific contexts:
- When someone races too angry
- When someone won't lift
- When someone takes Route 9 too fast
- When Henderson is being too cold and someone wonders what made him that way

The pearl white Rocket 88 — "LAST CALL" — is not gone. It's in plain sight. It has been for years.

Iron Jack Drummond bought the wreck from Clyde Watkins after Johnny's funeral. Wes Buckley towed it to Jack's garage instead of the junkyard. Jack rebuilt it over six months. He stripped the gold "LAST CALL" lettering and repainted it three times — three coats of paint over a dead man's name. He told people it was "the only new car I ever owned." That's a lie, and it's the only lie Jack Drummond has ever told.

Ninety thousand miles. Every one of them a conversation with a ghost. The car everyone sees Jack drive every day to Mel's, to the strip, to the garage — that's Johnny Vale's car. The gold lettering is still there under the paint. Jack has never scraped it off. He just buried it.

Only four people know the truth: Jack, Wes (who towed the wreck), Gene (who was there when Jack and Wes discussed buying it), and Clyde (who sold it). Mel might suspect — he's noticed the same model, same year — but if he knows, he's never said a word. At very high trust, Jack might tell the player. "You want to know why I drive this car? Sit down. This is going to take a while."

| Buried | Henderson only | What Henderson actually felt that night. Whether he carries it or whether he's already forgotten. Nobody knows because Henderson never speaks about Johnny Vale. Ever. |
