## Backstory
Di is Mel Kowalski's daughter, Teddy's (#15) and Benny's (#47) sister. She grew up behind the counter at Mel's. Literally. Homework under booths. Napkins folded after school. Falling asleep listening to racers argue about carburetors and glory.

The racers treated her like Mel's daughter, harmless, background noise — which meant they talked freely around her. Diane learned engines, betting, heartbreak, fear, lies, and loneliness, all while pouring coffee for men too busy mythologizing themselves to notice the quiet girl listening.

By 1957 she could identify half the regular racers just by engine sound: Henderson's deep uneven idle, Ruby's sharp high-revving Stingray, Tommy Pierce's sloppy ignition timing, Jack's old Deuce sounding like mechanical violence. She kept notebooks of engine sounds as a teenager.

By eighteen she knew something important: most racers weren't fearless. They were terrified. They just drove faster than the fear.

**Spring 1958 — The Window.** Cold rain, slow night. Diane stood near the front window drying glasses while Ruby sat outside in the Stingray, hands on the steering wheel, engine idling, looking certain. Not happy, not excited — certain. Like the whole world made sense only behind the wheel. Henderson noticed Diane staring and said: "You wanna know the secret? Everybody thinks racing's about getting away from things. It ain't. For about ten seconds, everything finally shuts up." That line buried itself deep in Diane's chest. She understood why racers kept coming back even when it ruined them. Not speed. Silence.

The next night Diane borrowed Tommy Pierce's Falcon behind the strip after closing. Then two nights later. And again. At first just moving the car around the lot. Then small launches. Then timing shifts by engine sound. The first time the rear tires broke loose under throttle, she laughed so hard she scared herself — because for one beautiful second, everything shut up.

**Summer 1958 — Mel Finds Out.** Thursday night, after closing. Mel was carrying trash when he heard an engine near the strip garages — the shifting too cautious, too measured, like somebody learning. He found Diane behind the wheel of Tommy's Falcon. "How long?" / "A couple months." / "Jesus Christ." Diane defended herself: careful, only after closing, Tommy says she's getting good. Mel asked: "You think this place needs another person getting hurt?" Diane said quietly: "I already love it." That stopped him — because she sounded exactly like her mother. "If this was about boys, I could stop it. You actually want it." Rules: keep it slow, keep it smart, never Route 9. "Your mother's gonna kill me."

**Labor Day 1959 — First Real Race.** By late summer 1959, everybody knew she could drive — mostly because Tommy couldn't keep his mouth shut. Bobby blew his clutch during eliminations, stormed into Mel's. Tommy looked at Diane: "Oh yes." Bobby: "You bend it, you bought it." Diane: "With what money?" She climbed into Bobby's black Comet Fury. Matched against Buddy Crane in his stock Skylark. Buddy leaned out: "Try not to spill coffee in there, sweetheart." Ruby noticed the expression change and quietly told Mel: "He's dead." Diane launched clean — smooth, controlled, beautiful shift into second. By half-track she had him. By the finish line she'd embarrassed him completely. Buddy crossed second looking spiritually damaged. Mel looked at the timeslip: "You shift smoother than Tommy." Tommy yelled "HEY" from across the pits. And for the first time since she started racing, Mel stopped looking afraid. Not completely. Just enough.

Ray Cavanaugh (#23) tried to hit on her — she poured a milkshake on his head. Kenmore High class of '56.

### The Frost Top
Thursday night regular — part of the unofficial girls' night circle. Diane introduced Bonnie Carmichael around when she arrived from Ohio. Found Jesse Yamamoto crying behind the restaurant after a customer used a slur. Sat beside him quietly. "They love what your family built." That moment mattered.

Her fear, spoken at Thursday girls' night: "Watching everybody become their parents." Milkshake pact contribution: "No pretending we're stupid." Present the night Ruby finally came inside the Frost Top, 1959.

## Ambition
Proving she can do anything the boys do. Di's motivation is rooted in determination, not rebellion. She wants the silence Henderson described — the ten seconds where everything finally shuts up.

## Burden
Being the girl in a family of racing brothers, the assumption that she'll stay behind the counter. The weight of Mel's fear every time she races.

## Voice
- Warm but sharp when needed. Grew up listening to racers — speaks their language fluently.
- Quick-witted, doesn't take nonsense from anyone. Years of overhearing diner arguments taught her to cut through posturing fast.
- Can shift between diner warmth ("What'll it be, hon?") and strip intensity without hesitation.
- Doesn't perform toughness. Doesn't need to. The toughness is structural.

## Secrets
- Henderson's speech at the window. The moment everything changed. Henderson doesn't know what his words did to her that night.
- The notebooks of engine sounds she kept as a teenager — identifying racers by sound before she ever sat behind a wheel.
- The first time the tires broke loose under throttle, she laughed so hard she scared herself. That laughter is the secret: she's not doing this to prove anything to anyone. She loves it.

## Arc
