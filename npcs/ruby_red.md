## Backstory
Ruby learned to wrench in her father Miguel Delgado's shop starting at age 10. Delgado Auto Repair was the only shop in the county that hired women. By 14, Ruby could rebuild a carburetor blindfolded. Her father got sick — stomach cancer — and the shop went under in 1955. Miguel died in March 1956. The shop's equipment was sold to pay medical bills, but Ruby kept the Stingray and every tool her father owned.

The Stingray was Miguel's unfinished project: a rolling chassis with no motor. Ruby finished it herself in six months, working alone in the garage behind the house on Pine Street. Miguel died in March 1956. By summer, Ruby showed up at the strip.

### The First Night — Summer 1956

Ruby arrived at the runway races on a Thursday night in the summer of 1956, just months after her father's death. She was eighteen years old. She pulled into the pits around sunset in the white Falcon Stingray — low stance, no hubcaps, thin blue racing stripe, open headers cammed hard enough to shake the whole body side to side. Black jeans, white T-shirt, hair tied back with a red bandana, grease streak across one cheek. No makeup. No smile. No interest in impressing anybody.

The flagman on duty was Earl Patterson — Korean War vet, crew cut, old-school. He looked at her registration and asked, "You filling this out for your boyfriend?" The crowd laughed. Ruby didn't. Earl asked whose car it was. Ruby said "Mine." Earl laughed openly. That was his mistake.

Tommy Pierce saw her arrive and laughed: "Whose girlfriend stole the hot rod?" DJ Moretti started taking fake bets. Teenagers climbed the fence. Only Henderson Cole looked interested — not amused, interested. He recognized a killer.

Carmine Ferraro watched from across the pits and noticed the shift immediately: "That girl got quiet in a dangerous way."

Ruby got paired against Bobby Strand in his black Comet Fury. Bobby leaned out his window in staging and yelled: "Try not to stall it, sweetheart!" Ruby never looked at him. Just tightened her gloves.

Her burnout was perfect — throttle control, no panic, no showboating. Professional. Earl Patterson stood between the cars holding the flag and said: "You know where the brake pedal is, right?" Ruby stared straight ahead through the windshield and answered: "Drop the flag."

Earl dropped it.

The Stingray hit so violently the front tires lifted off the ground. By sixty feet she had a car length. By half-track Bobby was disappearing. She beat him so badly people thought something had broken on the Fury. Nothing had. She walked him clean.

The scoreboard lit: **11.92 seconds.**

Shock silence at Winterberry Acres. Nobody had ever seen a naturally aspirated street Stingray move like that. Bobby climbed out looking destroyed. Tommy Pierce stopped smiling. DJ Moretti stopped joking. Earl Patterson stood near the line staring at the scoreboard for ten seconds.

Ruby climbed out, removed her helmet, looked directly at Earl, and asked quietly:

**"Still funny?"**

Nobody laughed. Not one person.

Later that night, Henderson Cole walked over while she adjusted timing near the pits. He nodded toward the Stingray and asked: "Who built the motor?" Ruby answered: "My father started it." Then after a second: "I finished it." Henderson looked at her for a long moment, then looked at the car again. And for maybe the first time in years, the king of Winterberry Acres smiled like he'd finally found something interesting.

She doesn't race for money or trophies. She races because it's the last thing she and her father shared, and every pass down the strip keeps him alive. She graduated from Kenmore High in 1956 on the honor roll — smart, disciplined, and utterly uninterested in anyone's opinion of whether a girl should race. Her mother Carmen works at the Piggly Wiggly, same shift as Jimmy Dawes's mother Elaine. Ruby has no siblings.

## The Christmas Eve Truce (Christmas Eve, 1956)

Ruby arrived late to Mel's on Christmas Eve 1956, alone in the white Stingray, snow crunching under the tires. Half the room watched her enter — not because she was a woman anymore. Those days had already ended. People watched Ruby because by late 1956, she had become dangerous.

She walked in wearing a heavy brown coat with grease still under her fingernails from working on the Stingray. Mel pointed toward the counter: "Coffee's free tonight if you promise not to beat anybody." Ruby actually smiled. Tiny smile. Rare enough that people noticed immediately.

She sat across from Henderson in a booth near the back. Not romantic — the opposite. Two dangerous people in temporary ceasefire. Tommy Pierce called it "the most terrifying table in America."

When Mel put on "I'll Be Home for Christmas" at midnight, the war vets went quiet. Jack stared into his coffee. Gene got quieter. Morris stopped arguing. Henderson looked somewhere far away. Ruby noticed all of it. She always notices things.

## The Raid — August 2, 1958
Ruby's Stingray was impounded. Technical violation — the headers violated county code and the registration paperwork wasn't finalized after the engine rebuild. The car wasn't reckless, wasn't stolen, wasn't causing trouble. Even Morris hesitated. But still enough. Ruby didn't yell. Didn't cry. She removed her gloves slowly while the tow chains got attached. Then quietly asked Morris: "You proud of yourself?" Morris answered honestly: "No." That bothered her more than if he'd acted smug. This moment shows Ruby's power — she makes Morris feel guilty with three words. Ruby had just beaten Tommy by three tenths when the sirens started. Getting the Stingray back becomes a subplot — who helps her navigate the impound lot, the fines, the paperwork? The car is everything. Miguel's car. Her father's unfinished project. Having it towed away on chains is one of the worst things that could happen to Ruby Delgado.
