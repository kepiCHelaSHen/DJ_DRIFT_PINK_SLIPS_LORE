# Crybaby Bridge

In the backroads outside town, where the trees swallowed the moonlight and the river carved a black scar through the ravine, there stood an old wooden crossing locals called Crybaby Bridge.

Built sometime in the 1830s, the bridge was never meant for speed. It was narrow. One lane only. Rotten planks held together by rusted iron bolts and prayers. During the day, farmers crossed it with trucks and feed wagons. But at night? It belonged to racers.

By the late summer of 1949, street racing had become a sickness in the county. Kids with flathead V8s, stripped coupes, and more courage than sense tore through the winding roads after dark looking for bragging rights. Nobody wanted to race Crybaby Bridge.

Except two drivers who thought they were untouchable.

The stories never fully agreed on who they were. Some say one was a local farm boy with a blacked-out Drifter. Others swear the second driver was an outsider from Cleveland or Pittsburgh—someone nobody had seen before and nobody saw again afterward. That part changes depending on who's telling it.

But everyone agrees on the night.

10:30 PM. Humid air. Thin fog hanging low over the river. The sound of engines echoing through the woods like thunder.

The two cars came screaming toward the bridge from opposite directions, both pushing hard, both refusing to lift.

Then headlights appeared.

A third car was crossing the bridge.

One racer slammed the brakes just in time, fishtailing sideways before stopping inches from the entrance. The other tried to thread the gap at full speed.

He never made it.

The old bridge groaned once—loud enough that people half a mile away claimed they heard it—and the car tore through the rotted guardrail before plunging into the ravine below.

The crash shook the valley.

Then came the sound.

Witnesses described steam hissing from the ruined engine. Coolant boiling. Twisted metal scraping against rock. A high-pitched wail echoing up from the darkness beneath the bridge.

Not human.

Not mechanical.

Something in between.

A crying sound.

By the time anyone climbed down into the ravine, the wreck was barely recognizable. Oil and gasoline coated the creek. The engine block was split open. But the strangest part?

No body was ever found.

No blood.
No bones.
Nothing.

The surviving racer disappeared before sunrise. Some say he fled town to avoid prison. Others say he swore something climbed out of the wreck before he ran.

Within weeks, locals stopped calling it Miller's Crossing.

It became Crybaby Bridge.

And even now, racers say if you idle too long at the center of the bridge after midnight, you can still hear it beneath the boards:

An engine hissing in the dark.

And something crying from the ravine below.
