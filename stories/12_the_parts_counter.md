# The Parts Counter

By 1958, everybody in Potter County knew two things about Valley Auto Supply.

First:
If Valley didn't have the part, you probably didn't need it.

Second:
If Lindsay Smith was behind the counter, you better know exactly what you were asking for.

Because Lindsay remembered everything.

Part numbers.
Gasket dimensions.
Bearing sizes.
Transmission fitments.
Spark plug heat ranges.
Who bought what.
Who still owed money.
Who lied about what broke.

People joked she had a catalog where normal humans kept emotions.

The scary part?

They weren't completely joking.

Valley Auto Supply sat just off Main Street squeezed between a hardware store and a laundromat that always smelled faintly like bleach and wet concrete. The building itself wasn't much:

faded blue paint
dusty front windows
warped wooden floors
shelves stacked to the ceiling

But racers treated it like church.

Because when something broke Friday afternoon before race night, Valley became the center of the universe.

And Lindsay ruled the parts counter like an air traffic controller during wartime.

Fast.
Precise.
No patience for nonsense.

If you walked in asking for:
"That little metal thing near the carburetor?"

She'd stare at you until shame physically entered your bloodstream.

The night everybody remembers happened in September of '58.

Friday.
5:42 PM.
Thirty-eight minutes before closing.

Which meant Lindsay was already irritated.

Rain hammered Main Street hard enough to blur headlights outside while the bell over the front door jingled nonstop from racers panic-buying parts before the weekend.

Somebody needed slicks.
Tommy Pierce needed belts.
DJ Moretti somehow needed another distributor despite allegedly replacing one three days earlier.

Normal chaos.

Then Bobby Strand came through the door.

And the whole atmosphere shifted.

Because Bobby looked bad.

Not injured.

Desperate.

Hair soaked from rain.
Grease on his jacket.
Breathing hard.

Even Lindsay looked up immediately.

That mattered.

Bobby slammed both palms on the counter.

"I need a starter."

Lindsay kept writing on an invoice.

"What kind?"

"Delco."

She didn't even blink.

"That narrows it down to about two hundred possibilities."

Bobby swore under his breath.

"Comet Fury. '56."

"Manual or automatic?"

"Manual."

"312?"

"Yes."

"Three-speed or four?"

"Four."

Lindsay finally looked up.

"See? We're communicating now."

Normally Bobby would've mouthed off.

Tonight he didn't.

Because everybody at the strip knew what was happening that evening.

Bobby had a grudge race against Henderson Cole.

Big money.
Packed crowd expected.
Probably the most important race Bobby would ever run.

And apparently his starter had just died.

Lindsay walked calmly into the back warehouse while rain hammered the roof overhead.

Whole store watching.

Tommy Pierce whispered:
"He's screwed if she doesn't have one."

DJ answered:
"She's got one. Valley always has one."

That was true.

Lindsay had inventory knowledge bordering on supernatural.

She disappeared between shelves for maybe thirty seconds.

Then returned holding a rebuilt Delco starter still wrapped in brown paper.

Correct part.
Correct housing.
Correct mounting pattern.

Bobby visibly relaxed.

"How much?"

Lindsay checked the invoice book.

Then:
"Thirty-two dollars."

Bobby froze.

Because everybody there knew Bobby didn't have thirty-two dollars.

Not after:

gambling losses
transmission rebuilds
missed shifts at the mill
and money owed to Tony Marcone

Bobby swallowed hard.

"I can pay Tuesday."

Lindsay said nothing.

That silence got heavy fast.

Because unlike Mel's Diner or the strip, Valley Auto Supply wasn't social territory.

This was business territory.

And Lindsay remembered debts.

All of them.

Bobby tried again:
"C'mon, Lindsay."

Still nothing.

Then she calmly opened the invoice ledger sitting beside the register.

Flip.
Flip.
Flip.

Stopped on Bobby Strand's page.

Then read aloud:

unpaid battery cables
overdue clutch assembly
borrowed timing light never returned
fuel pump from June
brake shoes from July

Tommy Pierce physically backed away from the counter.

Because hearing your debts spoken out loud by Lindsay felt weirdly similar to being read your crimes in church.

Bobby looked around the store embarrassed.

"Okay. I get it."

"No," Lindsay replied calmly.

"You don't."

That shut the whole room down.

Rain pounding outside.
Store silent inside.

Lindsay leaned forward slightly over the counter.

"You think this place stays open because everybody eventually 'gets me Tuesday'?"

Bobby didn't answer.

Because she was right.

Everybody in Potter County lived on credit.
But somebody always paid eventually or the whole system collapsed.

And Lindsay knew exactly how close to collapse most racers already were.

Bobby rubbed water from his face frustrated.

"If I miss tonight, Henderson'll never let me hear the end of it."

Lindsay answered immediately:
"That sounds like a Bobby problem."

Even DJ stopped smiling after that.

Because now everybody realized:
Lindsay wasn't being cruel.

She was being immovable.

Huge difference.

Then Bobby made the mistake.

"Thought maybe you'd help somebody local."

Lindsay stared at him for several seconds.

Then quietly:
"I did."

Bobby frowned.

"When?"

"Every time I let you walk outta here without paying."

Silence again.

That one landed.

Hard.

Because everybody in the store suddenly realized Lindsay had already BEEN helping him for months.

Bobby just never noticed because racers mistake survival for entitlement all the time.

Bobby looked exhausted after that.

Not angry anymore.
Just beaten.

Finally he muttered:
"So what, I'm done?"

Lindsay looked at the starter in her hands.

Then toward the rain outside.

Then back at Bobby.

And something in her expression shifted slightly.

Not softer.

Thoughtful.

Because despite everything, Lindsay loved racers.

Even the stupid ones.

Especially the stupid ones.

Finally she asked:
"You racing Henderson clean?"

Bobby blinked.

"What?"

"No side bets. No Tony money. No borrowed cash. Clean race."

Bobby hesitated.

That hesitation told her everything.

Lindsay nodded slowly.

"Then no."

Whole store froze.

Bobby stared at her in disbelief.

"You're serious?"

"Yes."

"You'd really let me miss the race?"

Lindsay slid the starter back beneath the counter.

"I'd rather let you miss one race than watch Tony Marcone own the next ten years of your life."

Nobody spoke after that.

Because everybody there knew somebody Tony already owned.

Maybe several somebodies.

Bobby stood motionless for a long moment while rain hammered the windows.

Then finally:
"You think Henderson ever worries like this?"

Lindsay answered instantly.

"Henderson worries exactly like this."

That surprised people.

Probably because it was true.

After another long silence, Bobby slowly reached into his jacket…
…and removed a folded envelope.

Cash.

Enough cash.

Lindsay raised one eyebrow.

"Thought you said Tuesday."

Bobby looked embarrassed.

"That was emergency money."

"For what?"

Bobby glanced toward the rain outside.

Then quietly:
"Rent."

Nobody moved.

Lindsay stared at him for several seconds.

Then took the money.

Not all of it.

Just enough for the starter.

Then pushed the rest back across the counter.

Bobby frowned.

"You said—"

"I said no credit."

She slid the boxed starter toward him.

"Not eviction."

That was the moment people understood something important about Lindsay Smith:

She wasn't hard because she lacked compassion.

She was hard because compassion without limits destroys people.

Bobby grabbed the starter and hurried toward the door.

Then paused before leaving.

Turned back toward Lindsay.

"Hey."

She looked up.

Bobby nodded once toward the counter.

"…thanks."

Lindsay was already writing another invoice.

"Beat Henderson and we'll call it even."

Bobby lost by two car lengths that night.

But years later he still told people:
"The smartest person at Winterberry Acres never drove the fastest car."

Then he'd grin slightly and add:

"She just sold the parts for it."
