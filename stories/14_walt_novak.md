# Walt Novak

The first time Lindsay Smith noticed Walt Novak, he was buying piston rings he technically shouldn't have known existed.

That was usually how she identified interesting people.

Not by clothes.
Not by confidence.
Not by stories.

By parts.

It happened late October, 1958.

Cold rain outside.
Friday afternoon rush inside Valley Auto Supply.
Half the county trying to fix race cars before Saturday night.

Lindsay was moving fast behind the counter:

invoices
distributor caps
timing chains
fuel filters
listening to three conversations simultaneously

Normal chaos.

Then the bell above the front door jingled and a man she'd never seen before walked in carrying a folded paper sack under one arm.

Average height.
Dark wool coat.
Work boots.
Quiet eyes.

Nothing flashy about him.

Which immediately made him stand out.

Because racers usually entered rooms like they were announcing boxing matches.

This guy entered like he hoped nobody noticed him.

Lindsay noticed him instantly anyway.

The stranger waited patiently while Tommy Pierce argued about spark plugs for nearly five straight minutes. Didn't interrupt once.

Another unusual thing.

Most men at Valley Auto Supply treated patience like a communicable disease.

Finally Lindsay looked up.

"What do you need?"

The stranger unfolded a small piece of paper carefully.

"Looking for Hastings forty-thousand-over moly rings."

Lindsay blinked once.

Not visibly.
Internally.

Because normal customers didn't ask for:

exact ring material
exact oversize
exact manufacturer

Normal customers said things like:
"Got piston stuff for a Falcon?"

Lindsay studied him quietly.

"What engine?"

"312 Y-block."

"Street or strip?"

Tiny pause.

"Mostly street."

That answer was a lie.

Not a huge lie.
Just enough of one.

Lindsay already knew it from the parts request alone.

Nobody bought moly rings for "mostly street" unless they planned on turning the engine into violence later.

She turned toward the shelves.

"Who's building it?"

"Friend of mine."

Another lie.

Again — not huge.

Just practiced.

That interested her more than the parts.

Lindsay found the rings without looking at the catalog and set the box on the counter.

"Those don't fit factory pistons."

The stranger nodded.

"I know."

"You decking the block too?"

Another pause.

Then:
"Maybe."

That finally confirmed it.

This man absolutely knew engines.

More than most racers at Winterberry Acres probably did.

But he was hiding it intentionally.

Lindsay leaned against the counter slightly.

"You from out east?"

That surprised him.

"How'd you know?"

"Accent."

He smiled faintly for the first time.

Small smile.
Tired smile.

"Pennsylvania."

"Steel?"

"Machining."

That fit immediately.

His hands gave it away too:

scarred knuckles
tiny burn marks
clean fingernails despite shop wear

Precision-worker hands.

Not racer hands.

At least not openly.

Lindsay rang up the parts.

Then casually:
"You balancing the rotating assembly yourself?"

The stranger looked up sharply.

There it was.

Recognition.

Not attraction yet.
Not trust.

Recognition.

Like realizing somebody else speaks the same strange language you do.

"Probably," he answered carefully.

Lindsay nodded once.

"Good. Most people don't bother."

The man glanced around the store briefly before asking:
"You race?"

That almost made Lindsay laugh.

Because everybody in Potter County knew exactly who she was by then.

Meaning:

he truly was new
or
he genuinely didn't care about local mythology

Either possibility interested her.

"Sometimes."

Tommy Pierce nearly choked hearing that answer from across the store.

The stranger noticed Tommy's reaction immediately.

Then looked back toward Lindsay slowly.

"You're Lindsay Smith."

Not a question.

Lindsay shrugged.

"That bad already?"

Tiny smile again from the stranger.

"No."

He picked up the parts box.

"Actually explains a lot."

Now she was curious.

"What's that supposed to mean?"

He hesitated.

Like he almost decided not to answer.

Then:
"You noticed the ring material before you noticed me."

That line landed harder than he probably intended.

Because he was right.

Most men noticed Lindsay as:

a woman
a racer
a challenge
a problem

This man noticed how her brain worked.

That was different.

The stranger reached for his wallet.

Lindsay glanced at the invoice form.

"Name?"

"Walter Novak."

"Walter?"

He winced slightly.

"Walt."

"Good. Walter sounds like somebody who sells insurance."

That earned an actual laugh.

Low laugh.
Unexpectedly warm.

Lindsay handed over the receipt.

"You building this motor alone?"

Walt folded the invoice carefully before answering.

"Trying to."

That answer carried weight.

Too much weight for a simple engine rebuild.

Lindsay noticed immediately.

She noticed everything.

Then the bell over the door jingled again as another wave of racers came inside escaping the rain.

The moment broke.

Walt stepped aside with his parts while Lindsay handled the growing line.

But before leaving, he stopped near the doorway.

Turned back toward the counter.

Then:
"You know… most people hear 'machinist' and assume factory work."

Lindsay kept writing an invoice.

"Most people are usually wrong."

Another pause.

Then Walt nodded once like she'd just answered a much larger question.

After he left, Tommy Pierce wandered toward the counter smirking.

"You flirting now?"

Lindsay didn't look up.

"No."

Tommy grinned.

"Looked like flirting."

Lindsay tore the invoice from the pad.

"No."

Then after a second:
"That was engine talk."

Tommy laughed.

"Pretty sure for you that's the same thing."

The scary part?

He might've been right.

Over the next few months Walt Novak kept appearing around Winterberry Acres quietly:

buying precision tools
obscure bearings
machining compounds
custom jets
never bragging
never racing
never volunteering information

Which only made Lindsay more interested.

Because men who actually knew things usually couldn't wait to tell everybody.

Walt hid competence like it was dangerous.

And eventually Lindsay realized something unsettling:

He wasn't hiding skill because he lacked confidence.

He was hiding it because somewhere before Winterberry Acres, being underestimated had kept him alive.
