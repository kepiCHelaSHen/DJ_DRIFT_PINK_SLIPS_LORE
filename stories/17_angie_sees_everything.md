# Angie Sees Everything

People talked differently around Angie Ferraro.

That was the dangerous part.

Because Angie didn't LOOK dangerous.

She looked like:

somebody's aunt
somebody's sister
somebody who remembered your coffee order

Warm smile.
Apron.
Hair scarf.
Grease stains from the grill.

Comfortable.

Which meant racers forgot she was always listening.

And Angie remembered everything.

Who fought.
Who lied.
Who owed money.
Who cried in the parking lot after losses.
Who kissed who behind the bleachers.
Who drank too much before races.
Who wasn't coming home happy.

The hot dog stand sat in the perfect spot:
between the staging lanes and the grandstands.

Meaning everybody passed Angie eventually.

Especially late at night when guards came down.

The conversation Angie wished she never overheard happened September, 1958.

Cold evening.
Light crowd.
Wind carrying exhaust smoke low across the strip.

Around eleven-thirty Angie was cleaning the stand after most people already left.

Just:

wiping counters
stacking paper trays
counting receipts

Normal closing routine.

The strip itself had gone mostly quiet except for a few engines cooling near the garages.

That's when she heard Tony Marcone's voice.

Low.
Calm.
Close.

Angie froze instinctively.

Not because Tony yelled.

Because Tony never yelled.

She slowly looked through the serving window toward the dark space beside the storage sheds.

Tony stood there smoking beside Deputy Earl Morris.

That alone felt wrong immediately.

Very wrong.

Because Morris hated Tony.

And Tony distrusted police on principle.

The two men rarely stood within twenty feet of each other voluntarily.

But tonight they were talking quietly like exhausted soldiers.

Angie should've walked away.

Instead she stayed still behind the stand listening without meaning to.

Morris looked furious.

Tony looked tired.

Finally Morris said:
"You think I don't know what's happening out here?"

Tony answered calmly:
"I think you know exactly what's happening."

Wind rattled the metal soda sign nearby.

Then Morris:
"They're kids."

Tony:
"Some are."

That silence afterward felt heavy.

Morris rubbed his face hard.

"I got bodies on Route 9 before the strip."

Tony nodded once.

"And fewer after."

That stopped Angie cold.

Because Tony was right.

Nobody wanted him to be right.
But he was.

Then Morris quietly said the sentence Angie carried for the rest of her life:

"One bad night and this whole town burns down around these boys."

Tony stared out toward the strip lights.

Then:
"Town was burning before they started racing."

That line shook Angie.

Because suddenly she understood something terrifying:

Neither man thought they were the villain.

Morris wanted control.
Tony wanted leverage.
Carmine wanted safety.
Henderson wanted respect.

And somehow all of them were feeding the same machine.

The wind shifted.

Tony noticed movement near the stand instantly.

That scared Angie more than anything.

He turned slightly toward the darkness.

Then:
"You hear something?"

Morris looked over.

Angie stopped breathing.

Long silence.

Then Tony finally shook his head.

"Probably nothing."

After a few more quiet words, the two men separated:

Morris toward the parking lot
Tony toward Main Street

Gone.

Angie remained motionless behind the stand for nearly a full minute afterward.

Not because she feared Tony.

Because she suddenly feared the future.

For the first time, Angie realized Winterberry Acres might not survive success.

Too much:

money
ego
gambling
speed
desperation

building pressure every weekend.

And underneath all of it sat boys pretending they were immortal.

When Carmine came by later to help close up, he immediately noticed something wrong.

"You okay?"

Angie looked out toward the dark empty strip.

Then quietly asked:
"You ever think this place could get too big?"

Carmine didn't answer right away.

Which was answer enough.

That frightened her more than anything Tony Marcone ever said.
