# Lindsay and Ruby

Summer, 1958.

About a month after Lindsay beat Norm Bassett.

About two years after Ruby humiliated Bobby Strand on her first pass down the quarter mile.

People always assumed Lindsay and Ruby were friends immediately.

They weren't.

Not enemies either.

Just… orbiting each other.

At Winterberry Acres, most racers existed loudly:

bragging
arguing
posturing
storytelling

Ruby and Lindsay existed quietly.

That confused people.

The men at the strip kept trying to turn them into rivals because men in the 1950s only understood two categories for women around competition:

enemies
girlfriends

Ruby and Lindsay fit neither.

Mostly they just watched each other from opposite sides of the pits like two mechanics inspecting unfamiliar tools.

The first real conversation happened on a Wednesday night after closing.

No crowd.
No races.
No music.

Just work.

A summer storm had rolled through Potter County around dusk leaving the whole strip wet and shining under scattered floodlights. Most people already went home.

Carmine stayed late reviewing invoices in the tower.
Jack was helping Tommy rebuild a transmission.
DJ was supposedly "helping clean up," which mostly meant talking while other people worked.

Ruby was under the hood of the Stingray near the far pit fence changing plugs after a rough ignition stumble during trials.

Lindsay was there because Norm Bassett forgot to pick up a parts shipment, so Valley Auto Supply dumped the delivery on her instead.

Normal bad luck.

The strip felt strange empty.

Without crowds and engines screaming, Winterberry Acres looked smaller.
Older.
Almost tired.

Lindsay finished unloading the last crate near the garage and noticed Ruby alone under the floodlights.

Most people left Ruby alone intentionally.

Not because she was mean.

Because she gave off the energy of somebody who preferred silence over company.

Lindsay respected that.

Which is probably why she walked over anyway.

Ruby heard the footsteps and glanced up briefly before going back to work.

Lindsay stopped beside the Stingray.

Looked into the engine bay.

Then:
"You're running rich."

Ruby didn't look up.

"I know."

"You gonna fix it?"

"Working on it."

Silence.

Lindsay nodded once.

Then:
"Passenger-side rear barrel's sticking."

That finally got Ruby's attention.

She straightened slightly.

"What?"

Lindsay pointed toward the carb linkage.

"Binding when the throttle snaps closed."

Ruby checked it.

Then paused.

Because Lindsay was right.

Tiny hitch in the linkage.
Easy to miss.
Enough to screw fuel balance under load.

Ruby adjusted it once manually.
The movement smoothed immediately.

That mattered.

Because Ruby hated being helped.

Mostly because most help came attached to ego.

Lindsay didn't seem interested in credit.

She just leaned against the pit fence quietly while Ruby worked.

Finally Ruby asked:
"How'd you catch that?"

Lindsay shrugged.

"Sounded wrong."

That answer made perfect sense to Ruby.

Engine people understood each other differently than normal people did.

Silence again.

Rainwater dripped from the edge of the tower roof somewhere nearby.

Far off, thunder rolled toward Riverside.

Finally Lindsay nodded toward the Stingray.

"Still the best launch at the strip."

Ruby kept adjusting the linkage.

"You saying yours isn't faster?"

Tiny smile from Lindsay.

"Mine's smarter."

That got the first actual smile Ruby had given anybody all week.

Small smile.
Fast smile.
Gone almost immediately.

But Lindsay noticed it.

Then came the real conversation.

Not about cars.

About everything underneath them.

Ruby closed the hood carefully.

Then leaned against the fender looking out toward the empty strip.

"You ever get tired of it?"

Lindsay frowned slightly.

"Of what?"

Ruby gestured vaguely toward the world:

the pits
the garages
the stares
the constant proving

"All of it."

Lindsay thought about the question seriously.

That mattered too.

Most people answered too quickly around Ruby.

Finally:
"No."

Ruby looked surprised.

Lindsay crossed her arms.

"I get tired of idiots."

That made Ruby laugh quietly.

Real laugh this time.

Rare enough that DJ Moretti later swore hearing Ruby laugh was like spotting a mountain lion in town.

Lindsay continued:
"But not this."

She nodded toward the strip.

"The cars don't care who you are."

That line hung in the humid air between them.

Because both women knew it was true.

Engines didn't care:

if you were poor
rich
male
female
respected
mocked

Cars only cared whether you understood them.

Ruby looked out toward the dark quarter mile for a long moment before quietly saying:
"People care though."

Lindsay nodded.

"Yeah."

Another silence.

Then Lindsay added:
"Which is why you gotta beat them so bad they stop having opinions."

Ruby actually grinned at that one.

Dangerous grin.
The kind Henderson got before races.

Then Ruby asked:
"You always this friendly?"

Lindsay:
"No."

"Good."

"Would've ruined my reputation."

That earned another laugh.

Then the moment shifted slightly.

Softer.

More honest.

Ruby finally asked the thing she'd been wondering for months:

"How bad was it for you?"

Lindsay understood immediately.

Not the racing.

The wall.

The constant resistance.
The jokes.
The dismissal.
The exhaustion of existing inside rooms that assumed you were temporary.

Lindsay leaned back against the fence thinking.

Then:
"Norm was worse than the racers."

Ruby nodded immediately.

"Yeah."

"Racers yell at you."

Lindsay glanced toward the garages.

"Mechanics explain your own car to you."

That one hit hard.

Because Ruby had lived it too.

Men describing engines she built.
Men questioning timeslips she earned.
Men assuming success belonged to fathers, boyfriends, or luck.

Never skill.

Ruby stared at the Stingray quietly.

Then:
"I used to keep records."

Lindsay looked over.

"Records?"

"Every stupid thing somebody said to me at the strip."

Lindsay blinked once.

"You serious?"

Ruby nodded.

"Filled two notebooks."

Lindsay laughed hard enough she had to look away.

Not mocking.

Understanding.

Because every woman who survived places like Winterberry Acres developed strange survival habits.

Finally Lindsay asked:
"What'd you do with the notebooks?"

Ruby looked out toward the empty track.

Then:
"Started winning too much."

That line became legendary later.

People repeated it for years.

But almost nobody knew where it came from.

The conversation lasted another hour after that.

Mostly:

cars
racers
stupid men
carburetors
fear
money
how exhausting respect could become

At one point Ruby admitted:
"Henderson listens better than most of them."

Lindsay nodded immediately.

"Because Henderson only respects competence."

"Still annoying."

"Obviously."

By the time they finally left, the storm had fully cleared.

Cool air.
Wet pavement.
Town lights glowing in the valley below.

Ruby grabbed her jacket from inside the Stingray.

Then paused before getting in.

Looked at Lindsay for a second.

Then:
"You wanna know the weird part?"

"What?"

Ruby glanced back toward the strip.

"They still think we're the unusual ones."

Lindsay smirked.

Then answered with the most Lindsay Smith sentence imaginable:

"Yeah."

She nodded toward the empty grandstands.

"Meanwhile they're the ones strapping themselves into explosions every weekend."

And for maybe the first time since either of them arrived at Winterberry Acres…

both women felt slightly less alone.
